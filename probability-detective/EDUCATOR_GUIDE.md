# Educator guide

## Objectives and flow

Learners distinguish theoretical probability from experimental proportion, explain small-sample variation, compare 10/100/1,000 trials, identify valid die outcomes and use expected points to reason about fairness. Reading simple decimals and multiplication is helpful; co-play is welcome. Suggested time: 10–15 minutes.

1. **Coins:** predict or explicitly skip, choose fair or labelled biased probability (including 0 and 1), then run 10, 100 or 1,000 trials. Counts, bars and table derive from one result. Copy states that more trials often approach theory without guaranteeing exact or monotonic improvement, and that a fair coin remains one half after a streak.
2. **Dice:** compare a fair die with two-dice sums. The worked scaffold lists six ordered pairs for 7 and one for 2, explaining why sums are unequal. Chart and table share generated counts.
3. **Game:** vary non-monetary point rewards. Expected advantage is probability times points. Simulation is compared with theory. The fresh two-dice challenge requires arithmetic: A has 6/36 times 2; B has 30/36 times 1.

Adapt by using only counts before decimals, running 10 and 1,000 side by side, listing die pairs with physical dice, or letting a co-player operate controls. Every answer can be retried; no timer, score or rank is used.

Offline follow-up: make a six-section paper spinner, predict ten spins, record results, then list its theoretical outcomes and explain one limitation of the small experiment.

## Sources and limitations

- [NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook, Probability Distributions](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda36.htm) supports the distinction between probability models and observed data distributions.
- [American Statistical Association, Pre-K–12 Guidelines for Assessment and Instruction in Statistics Education II](https://www.amstat.org/asa/education/Guidelines-for-Assessment-and-Instruction-in-Statistics-Education-Reports.aspx) supports using simulation, variability and evidence-based statistical reasoning.

The app models independent idealised coins/dice. A pseudo-random browser source is sufficient for learning simulation but not cryptography. Automated feedback checks structured choices and arithmetic; it does not infer arbitrary prose.
